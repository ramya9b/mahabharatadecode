Everyone knows the number — a hundred Kauravas against five Pandavas. Almost nobody remembers how the hundred actually arrived.

It was not a hundred cradles. According to the Mahabharata's Adi Parva, Gandhari's pregnancy dragged on for two years with no birth in sight. When she heard that Kunti had already had her first son, grief and jealousy got the better of her — she struck her own womb, and delivered a hard, lifeless ball of flesh.

She was about to throw it away. Then the sage Vyasa said: don't. Cut it, cool it with water, keep the pieces in pots of ghee, and wait. From those pots came Duryodhana, his ninety-nine brothers, and one daughter, Dushala.

## LinkedIn post
The birth of the hundred Kauravas is one of the most-skimmed passages in the Mahabharata — and one of the most quietly instructive.

The story, from the Adi Parva: Gandhari is promised a hundred sons. Her pregnancy stalls for two years. Then she hears that her sister-in-law Kunti has already given birth. It is the comparison, not the waiting, that breaks her — she strikes her own womb and delivers a lifeless mass of flesh.

The sage Vyasa salvages it, dividing the mass into a hundred and one pieces kept in pots of ghee, from which the sons and a daughter emerge. At the eldest's birth — Duryodhana — the omens are read as a plain warning. The father keeps him anyway, out of love.

Two leadership-adjacent ideas sit inside this myth:
1) The most dangerous moment in a long wait is the instant you measure yourself against a rival's timeline.
2) A warning ignored out of affection is still a warning ignored.
