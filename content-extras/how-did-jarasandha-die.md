Secondary intent includes understanding *why* he was so hard to kill (his birth in two halves), *why* he had to die (the Rajasuya and the captive kings), and *who* actually killed him (Bhima, not Krishna). ## LinkedIn Post
The death of Jarasandha is one of the best "strategy over strength" stories in ancient Indian literature.

Jarasandha, emperor of Magadha, was born from two halves fused together by a demoness — and his body kept re-sealing any wound. Bhima, the strongest man in the Mahabharata, wrestled him for days and could not finish him. Force wasn't the missing ingredient.

Krishna's contribution wasn't power. It was a reframe. Instead of asking "how do we hurt him more?" he asked "what does his body need in order to heal — and can we take that away?" He split a twig, threw the halves apart, and Bhima understood: tear the king in two and reverse the pieces so they can't rejoin.

A great strength and a great weakness are often the same trait, read from two directions. Worth a read if you like the moments where the epic solves an impossible problem with an idea rather than a weapon.
