And here's the part that keeps people arguing three thousand years later: Duryodhana was actually the *better* mace fighter. In a fair duel, he might have won. So why didn't he?

## LinkedIn post
There's a leadership question buried in the death of Duryodhana that still lands today.

On the last day of the Mahabharata war, Bhima defeated Duryodhana with a mace blow below the waist — a clear foul. Duryodhana was the more skilled fighter; a clean duel might have gone his way. The blow fulfilled a vow Bhima had sworn years earlier, after Duryodhana humiliated Draupadi.

What makes the passage remarkable is that the epic refuses to tidy it up. Krishna defends the foul: the other side had abandoned every rule first, so the rulebook no longer applied. Balarama, who trained both fighters, calls it a disgrace and leaves.

That argument is one many of us face in smaller forms: when the other side stops playing fair, does staying clean become a kind of surrender? The Mahabharata stages both answers and declines to declare a winner.
