## LinkedIn Post
We remember the heroes of the Mahabharata for what they did. We rarely remember the man whose only job was to see clearly and tell the truth.

Sanjaya was a suta — a charioteer and bard, not a royal — who became the blind king Dhritarashtra's most trusted counsellor. Before the war at Kurukshetra, the sage Vyasa offered to restore the king's eyesight so he could watch the battle. Dhritarashtra refused; he could not face seeing his own family destroyed. So the gift of divine sight passed to Sanjaya instead, and for eighteen days he narrated the entire war to the king who would not look.

Three things about him are worth carrying into any workplace:

1. He told power the truth it did not want. Sent as an envoy to the Pandavas, he came back and told the king plainly that his sons held neither the moral ground nor the likely victory.

2. He served without lying. He was loyal to Dhritarashtra and still praised the other side, because loyalty to a person did not mean betraying the facts.

3. He is the reason the Bhagavad Gita exists in words. The whole text is framed as his report — and he speaks its final verse himself.

The most valuable person in a room full of power is often not the most powerful one. It is the one who sees clearly and refuses to shade what they see.
